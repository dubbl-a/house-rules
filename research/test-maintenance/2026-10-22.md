# Test suite maintenance practices (2026-10-22)

Research note. It backs three new rules and two amendments in `plugins/house/modules/testing/rules/testing.md`; the handbook sections are in `docs/handbook/testing.md` and the inventory rows are EXT-119 to EXT-125 in `docs/handbook/inventory.md`.

## Question

What are current best practices for maintaining an automated test suite, according to modern authoritative sources (Google Testing Blog and Software Engineering at Google, Martin Fowler, ThoughtWorks Technology Radar, Kent Beck, the xUnit Test Patterns body of work, peer-reviewed software engineering research from roughly 2015 onward, and major engineering org blogs)? Scope: test smells and refactoring tests, fixture and test data management, when to delete or merge redundant or obsolete tests, keeping tests fast (sharding, parallelism, size, hermeticity), flaky test handling, test ownership and readability (DAMP vs DRY), and coverage as a maintenance signal.

## Depth

One standard-depth deep-research run: 6 angles, 73 agent calls, 20 sources fetched, 80 claims extracted. Only 15 of the 80 were verified (each by three votes), all 15 confirmed and none refuted. Every finding below rests on those 15, merged into 7. A source labeled unreliable by the run (the O'Reilly copy of the book, a Springer article, an older Google coverage post) supplied no verified claim.

## Findings

1. Aim for tests that change only when requirements change, by testing behavior through public interfaces. Confidence medium, vote 3-0 on two merged claims. Source: https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch12.html (Software Engineering at Google, chapter 12, 2020). Caveat: a single source, raised from low to medium because verifiers recalled the same principle elsewhere without fetching it; the book presents an ideal, and real suites also change for readability and flakiness fixes. Became rule A.
2. Prefer state testing over interaction testing, and a real object over a mock when it is fast and deterministic. Confidence low, vote 3-0. Source: same chapter. Caveat: one practitioner book, no empirical data; the mockist (London-school) tradition disagrees and uses interaction tests as a design tool, which comes from verifier notes and not a verified claim. Became the second paragraph of rule A.
3. Flakiness is common, costly, and persistent: in 2016 about 1.5% of Google test runs were flaky, almost 16% of tests had some flakiness, about 84% of pass-to-fail transitions involved a flaky test, and insertion of new flakiness was about as fast as fixing. Confidence low, vote 3-0 on three merged claims. Source: https://testing.googleblog.com/2016/05/flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we.html. Caveat: one 2016 Google post with no method for the insertion and fix rates; the author says Google does not track how often flaky tests hide real bugs; "manage, not eliminate" is the run's inference. Became amendment D.
4. Rank test smells by severity, not detector counts: developers rated 42 of 48 detected smells low severity, and rated Assertion Roulette, Eager Test, and Unknown Test most harmful. Confidence medium, vote 3-0 on three merged claims. Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.13902 and https://2020.msrconf.org/details/msr-2020-papers/14/Investigating-Severity-Thresholds-for-Test-Smells. Caveat: a six-project interview study, severity is perceived and not measured harm, and the 2021 study is a preprint. Became rule C.
5. Most real test deletions are obsolete tests removed with their code: about 91.4% in six of seven Java projects (24,431 confirmed deleted tests), about 7% redundant passing tests, about 1.6% failing tests. Confidence low, vote 3-0. Source: https://2025.msrconf.org/details/msr-2025-technical-papers/24/Understanding-Test-Deletion-in-Java-Applications. Caveat: one study of open-source Java; it describes what developers do, not proof of best practice. Became rule B.
6. Removing a test that looks redundant is not automatically safe: deleting 20% of the redundant tests reduced coverage or mutation scores, and the best reduction approach found up to 54% of the redundant tests developers deleted. Confidence low, vote 3-0 on two merged claims. Source: same MSR 2025 paper. Caveat: verifiers read only the abstract; it is unclear whether the 20% is a share of redundant tests or of cases, and the techniques evaluated are unconfirmed. Became rule B.
7. Coverage is useful on the individual change in code review but a weak effectiveness measure alone: 291 of 2,041 methods (14%) in one industrial project were pseudo-tested, and counting their lines as uncovered would lower line coverage from 89% to 82%. Confidence medium, vote 3-0 on three merged claims. Sources: https://research.google/pubs/code-coverage-at-google/ and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08480. Caveat: the pseudo-testing data is one project whose language was not confirmed; Google also computes project-level coverage daily; pairing coverage with mutation checks is the run's synthesis. Became amendment E.

## Caveats

- Most findings rest on a single source, which the run's rubric rates low. Only findings 1, 4, and 7 reach medium.
- The Google flakiness figures are a decade old and may have changed with later tooling.
- The deletion findings come from one 2025 paper read as an abstract.
- Nothing came from ThoughtWorks Technology Radar, Kent Beck, Martin Fowler's own pages, or the xUnit Test Patterns book, though the question named them; those appear only in verifier recall, never fetched.

## Gaps

No verified claim covered fixtures and test data management, sharding and parallelism, test size and hermeticity, test ownership, or DAMP vs DRY. This note gives no sourced recommendation on them and the testing module adds no rule on them.

## Open questions

- What do post-2020 industry sources recommend for flaky-test policy (quarantine, automatic retry, ownership-based deadlines, zero tolerance), and is there evidence on which policy keeps real failures from being ignored?
- Is there empirical evidence on DAMP vs DRY in test code: does helper and fixture abstraction raise or lower maintenance cost and defect detection?
- Is mutation testing, including pseudo-tested-method detection, cost-effective as a routine maintenance gate at scale, compared with changeset coverage in review?
- What do authoritative sources say about test size, hermeticity, and sharding as speed practices, and where do they disagree (Google's small, medium, large sizing against the test pyramid against the testing trophy)?
- To verify more of the 80 extracted claims, the run can be resumed with a larger verify budget (about 76 more agents for 40 claims).
