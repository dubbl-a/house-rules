# Test suite maintenance practices (2026-10-22)

Research note. It backs three new rules and two amendments in `plugins/house/modules/testing/rules/testing.md`; the handbook sections are in `docs/handbook/testing.md` and the inventory rows are EXT-119 to EXT-125 in `docs/handbook/inventory.md`.

## Question

What are current best practices for maintaining an automated test suite, according to modern authoritative sources (Google Testing Blog and Software Engineering at Google, Martin Fowler, ThoughtWorks Technology Radar, Kent Beck, the xUnit Test Patterns body of work, peer-reviewed software engineering research from roughly 2015 onward, and major engineering org blogs)? Scope: test smells and refactoring tests, fixture and test data management, when to delete or merge redundant or obsolete tests, keeping tests fast (sharding, parallelism, size, hermeticity), flaky test handling, test ownership and readability (DAMP vs DRY), and coverage as a maintenance signal.

## Depth

One standard-depth deep-research run: 6 angles, 73 agent calls, 20 sources fetched, 80 claims extracted. Only 15 of the 80 were verified (each by three votes), all 15 confirmed and none refuted. Every finding below rests on those 15, merged into 7. A source labeled unreliable by the run (the O'Reilly copy of the book, a Springer article, an older Google coverage post) supplied no verified claim.

## Findings

The confidence labels and numbers below are the run's own; the Corroboration section after Open questions corrects several of them from fetched sources, and where they differ the Corroboration section governs.

1. Aim for tests that change only when requirements change, by testing behavior through public interfaces. Confidence medium, vote 3-0 on two merged claims. Source: https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch12.html (Software Engineering at Google, chapter 12, 2020). Caveat: a single source, raised from low to medium because verifiers recalled the same principle elsewhere without fetching it; the book presents an ideal, and real suites also change for readability and flakiness fixes. Became rule A.
2. Prefer state testing over interaction testing, and a real object over a mock when it is fast and deterministic. Confidence low, vote 3-0. Source: same chapter. Caveat: one practitioner book, no empirical data; the mockist (London-school) tradition disagrees and uses interaction tests as a design tool, which comes from verifier notes and not a verified claim. Became the second paragraph of rule A.
3. Flakiness is common, costly, and persistent: in 2016 about 1.5% of Google test runs were flaky, almost 16% of tests had some flakiness, about 84% of pass-to-fail transitions involved a flaky test, and insertion of new flakiness was about as fast as fixing. Confidence low, vote 3-0 on three merged claims. Source: https://testing.googleblog.com/2016/05/flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we.html. Caveat: one 2016 Google post with no method for the insertion and fix rates; the author says Google does not track how often flaky tests hide real bugs; "manage, not eliminate" is the run's inference. Became amendment D.
4. Rank test smells by severity, not detector counts: developers rated 42 of 48 detected smells low severity, and rated Assertion Roulette, Eager Test, and Unknown Test most harmful. Confidence medium, vote 3-0 on three merged claims. Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.13902 and https://2020.msrconf.org/details/msr-2020-papers/14/Investigating-Severity-Thresholds-for-Test-Smells. Caveat: a six-project interview study, severity is perceived and not measured harm, and the 2021 study is a preprint. Became rule C.
5. Most real test deletions are obsolete tests removed with their code: about 91.4% in six of seven Java projects (24,431 confirmed deleted tests), about 7% redundant passing tests, about 1.6% failing tests. Confidence low, vote 3-0. Source: https://2025.msrconf.org/details/msr-2025-technical-papers/24/Understanding-Test-Deletion-in-Java-Applications. Caveat: one study of open-source Java; it describes what developers do, not proof of best practice. Became rule B.
6. Removing a test that looks redundant is not automatically safe: deleting 20% of the redundant tests reduced coverage or mutation scores, and the best reduction approach found up to 54% of the redundant tests developers deleted. Confidence low, vote 3-0 on two merged claims. Source: same MSR 2025 paper. Caveat: verifiers read only the abstract; it is unclear whether the 20% is a share of redundant tests or of cases, and the techniques evaluated are unconfirmed. Became rule B.
7. Coverage is useful on the individual change in code review but a weak effectiveness measure alone: 291 of 2,041 methods (14%) in one industrial project were pseudo-tested, and counting their lines as uncovered would lower line coverage from 89% to 82%. Confidence medium, vote 3-0 on three merged claims. Sources: https://research.google/pubs/code-coverage-at-google/ and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08480. Caveat: the pseudo-testing data is one project whose language was not confirmed; Google also computes project-level coverage daily; pairing coverage with mutation checks is the run's synthesis. Became amendment E.

## Caveats

- Most findings rest on a single source, which the run's rubric rates low. Only findings 1, 4, and 7 reach medium.
- The Google flakiness figures are a decade old and may have changed with later tooling.
- The deletion findings come from one 2025 paper read as an abstract.
- Nothing came from ThoughtWorks Technology Radar, Kent Beck, Martin Fowler's own pages, or the xUnit Test Patterns book, though the question named them; those appear only in verifier recall, never fetched.

## Gaps

No verified claim covered fixtures and test data management, sharding and parallelism, test size and hermeticity, test ownership, or DAMP vs DRY. This note gives no sourced recommendation on them and the testing module adds no rule on them.

## Open questions

- What do post-2020 industry sources recommend for flaky-test policy (quarantine, automatic retry, ownership-based deadlines, zero tolerance), and is there evidence on which policy keeps real failures from being ignored?
- Is there empirical evidence on DAMP vs DRY in test code: does helper and fixture abstraction raise or lower maintenance cost and defect detection?
- Is mutation testing, including pseudo-tested-method detection, cost-effective as a routine maintenance gate at scale, compared with changeset coverage in review?
- What do authoritative sources say about test size, hermeticity, and sharding as speed practices, and where do they disagree (Google's small, medium, large sizing against the test pyramid against the testing trophy)?
- To verify more of the 80 extracted claims, the run can be resumed with a larger verify budget (about 76 more agents for 40 claims).

## Corroboration

A second pass searched for independent sources per finding. "Fetched" means the page text was read; a snippet-only source is marked unverified and is not relied on.

### A. Behavior through the public interface; state over interaction (confidence medium)

- Supports, fetched: Fowler, "Mocks Aren't Stubs", https://martinfowler.com/articles/mocksArentStubs.html. A classic test "only cares about the final state - not how that state was derived"; mockist tests are "more coupled to the implementation of a method".
- Qualifies, fetched, same page: classicists "use real objects if possible and a double if it's awkward to use the real thing". Some behavior has no observable state ("The whole point of a cache is that you can't tell from its state whether the cache hit or missed"), so an interaction check is sometimes the only check.
- Qualifies, fetched: Fowler, https://martinfowler.com/bliki/UnitTest.html. Even a classic tester uses doubles for an awkward collaboration, which are "invaluable to remove non-determinism when talking to remote services".
- Google Testing on the Toilet posts on the same point are the same organization as the book, and only their comments were fetched, so they are not independent.
- Not supported independently: that over-reliance on mocking frameworks is the most common cause of brittle interaction tests. The handbook attributes it to the book alone and the rule drops it.
- Rule change applied: prefer a real collaborator when fast and deterministic; a double is right for an awkward or non-deterministic collaborator; an interaction assertion is the right check where there is no observable state.

### B. Deletion and redundancy (confidence low to medium)

The full MSR 2025 paper was read: https://hifromajay.github.io/papers/msr25.pdf.

- 24,431 deleted tests: confirmed. 91.4% obsolete: confirmed, but for six of seven projects; the excluded project held 70% of all deletions. 38% of obsolete tests were deleted in a later commit and 62% with their code.
- About 7% were redundant (518 tests), 92% of those from one project (pmd).
- The "20%" is not a deletion experiment. Of the 518 tests developers deleted as redundant, 103 each lowered line or branch coverage when removed individually, and 66 of 369 lowered the mutation score. Median loss was 5% line, 7% branch, 12% mutation. So about one redundant-judged deletion in five lost coverage.
- The "54%" is the best case of one family of similarity-based reduction tools (FAST-R) under a loose budget, pooled; a strict budget gave 8% on one project and no approach was consistent. The authors say the approaches can be used as a filtering step.
- Unverified (snippet only): Shi and others, FSE 2015, on reduction losing fault detection on changes.
- Deleting a test with its code is supported as practice. Naming the covering test and showing it fail on the defect is reasonable but not directly tested.
- Rule change applied: treat a reduction tool's output as a filter, not a verdict.

### C. Test smells (confidence medium to low)

- arXiv 2107.13902 (Campos and others) is a preprint; no venue found.
- Qualifies, fetched: Bavota and others, EMSE 2015, https://www.iris.unisa.it/handle/11386/4465858. Test smells have a strong negative impact on comprehension and maintenance (comprehension about 30% better without smells). "Most smells are low severity" is perception, not measured harm.
- Qualifies, fetched: Tufano and others, 2016. Developers often fail to recognize harmful smells.
- Contradicts detector-count triage, fetched: Panichella and others, EMSE 2022, https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/test-smells-20-years-later-detectability-validity-and-reliability. The current vocabulary of test smells is "highly mismatched to real concerns"; an older tool misclassified over 70% of test smells.
- Unverified (snippet only): Spadini and others, ICSME 2018, on Indirect Testing, Eager Test and Assertion Roulette as strongest for change-proneness.
- Nothing supports the no-assertion test as a priority.
- Rule change applied: prioritize the eager test and assertion roulette; treat a detector's output as unreliable triage input, neither a backlog nor a severity ranking; drop the no-assertion priority; do not imply most smells are harmless.

### D. Flaky tests (quarantine with owner and cadence medium to high; recurrence claim low)

- No independent confirmation of Google's 1.5%, 16%, 84% or of "inserted as fast as fixed"; present them as one organization's 2016 observation.
- Qualifies, fetched: Meta, https://engineering.fb.com/2020/12/10/developer-tools/probabilistic-flakiness/. All real-world tests are flaky to some extent; unit tests well below 1%, some end-to-end frameworks up to 10%; "a failure is merely a hint to run the test again"; a ticket goes to the declared owner of the test.
- Supports quarantine and uses retry, fetched: Gradle, https://blog.gradle.org/how-we-handle-flaky-tests-in-gradle. "Retry all failed tests, quarantine the too-flaky tests, and fix the flaky tests through teamwork"; flaky tests reviewed once per week.
- Supports quarantine, fetched: Fowler, https://martinfowler.com/articles/nonDeterminism.html. Quarantine, but fix quickly; quarantined tests get forgotten.
- Qualifies, fetched: Lam and others, ICSE 2020 (Microsoft), https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-study-on-the-lifecycle-of-flaky-tests/. Claimed fixes often did not fix or reduce flakiness.
- "Never retry" is contradicted as stated. Rule change applied: a retry is acceptable when a pass after a failure is recorded as a flake and routed to the quarantine list and its owner; what is ruled out is a retry nobody sees. The Don't bullet is reworded to match, the heading is unchanged, and a fix is confirmed by rerunning before a test leaves quarantine.

### E. Coverage (changeset coverage medium; covered is not tested, medium to high)

- Supports, fetched: Fowler, https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TestCoverage.html. A coverage level made a target will be attained by gaming; coverage is "a useful tool for finding untested parts of a codebase".
- Supports, fetched: Petrovic and others, Google, https://research.google/pubs/practical-mutation-testing-at-scale-a-view-from-google/. Mutating "only changed code during code review, rather than the entire code base".
- Qualifies, unverified (snippet only): a 2020 Google Testing Blog post calls coverage "a reasonable, objective, industry standard metric with actionable data", so "never a target" is stronger than Google's wording.
- Rule change applied: say a threshold set as the goal gets gamed rather than implying every source rejects coverage numbers; mention mutation on changed code in review as the practiced pairing.
